01

The verdict in one page

Your three pillars — missing-person identification, federated methodology, biometric privacy — are sound and they sit on a genuine research gap. What needs to change is which part of the model you federate, what data you train on, and how you measure success.

Do not federate the face recognition backbone. Freeze it, and federate two small modules on top of it. That single decision is what turns this from a research programme into a capstone.

An ArcFace backbone is trained on 10 million+ images across 100,000+ identities with a classification head spanning the whole identity set. Nothing at a hospital or college node resembles that. Federating it would collapse the embedding space within a handful of rounds, and the 166 MB payload would make secure aggregation and differential privacy prohibitively expensive on top.

Freeze it instead. Federate a surveillance adapter (≈0.4 M parameters) that corrects for each site's camera degradation, and a match verifier (≈0.13 M parameters) trained on labels your node operators produce when they confirm or reject an alert. Total federated payload drops to about 2 MB per round — small enough that masking, noise, and bandwidth all become non-issues.

Reviewers will ask why you cannot just centralise. Your answer is not "privacy law" — it is a technical fact about the data:

The most valuable training signal at each node is its own hard negatives — the thousands of ordinary people who walk past that camera, are not the missing person, and yet look similar once compressed to a 30-pixel face. Those faces are exactly the ones that can never be centralised: they are uninvolved bystanders who never consented to anything. Federated learning is not a privacy garnish here. It is the only way to learn from the data that matters most.

02

Audit of your draft plan

Every proposal from your brief, with a verdict and a replacement where needed. Nothing here is discarded without a substitute.

Rework "Fine-tune a pretrained model with LFW, CelebA and custom CCTV."

LFW and CelebA are high-resolution, well-lit, near-frontal celebrity photographs. CCTV faces are 20–50 pixels between the eyes, off-axis, motion-blurred and H.264-compressed. A model tuned on LFW learns nothing about that domain, and paper [2]'s 99.65% on LFW will not survive the transfer.

Instead: keep the backbone frozen and train a small adapter on synthetically degraded pairs, then validate that the adapter transfers to natively-low-resolution surveillance data (QMUL-SurvFace, TinyFace). Use LFW only to report one comparable verification number against [2] and [6].

Drop "The central server improves from node weights" — i.e. federating the whole recognition model.

Not achievable at capstone scale, for the reasons in §01. The margin-based losses that make ArcFace work (paper [1], [7]) require a classifier over the global identity set; a node holding a handful of identities cannot compute a meaningful gradient for it.

Instead: federate the adapter and the verifier only. Everything else is a frozen, globally-shared, publicly-available checkpoint — which also means no node ever needs to download a model your project trained from scratch.

Rework "Based on this local model updates its weights" — training triggered by a match.

A single match is one sample of one identity. That is not a training signal for recognition, and treating it as one would cause the node's model to drift toward whoever it saw last.

Instead: an operator-confirmed match or rejection is a clean binary label. Accumulate those into a local buffer and train the verifier head on them, alongside hard negatives mined from that node's own non-matching tracks. That is a real, defensible learning loop — and it is what your node dashboard is actually for.

Rework "Server sends the missing person photo, maybe by adding salt."

Salting does not apply to images. A salt randomises a hash input; it does not hide a face, and any pixel perturbation large enough to obscure identity also destroys the biometric.

Instead: distribute a cancelable biometric template in the style of DeepShieldFed [2] — select the top-k most discriminative embedding dimensions, quantise by mean thresholding to a binary code, and XOR-mask it with a key derived from the case ID and the destination node ID. The template is revocable, per-node unlinkable, and useless if the node's database leaks.

Also note, honestly, in your report: in real missing-person cases the probe photograph is usually already public on police portals and news media. The privacy that genuinely needs defending is the bystanders'.

Rework "Nodes don't share matched photos."

If a node finds the missing person and sends only a similarity score, the alert is unactionable — no one can dispatch a response to a number.

Instead: two-stage disclosure. The local operator reviews the candidate first; only a confirmed alert releases a minimal payload — one cropped face, camera ID, timestamp, and confidence. Everything else, which is 99.99% of what the camera sees, never leaves the node and is deleted within seconds. Frame this in your report as purpose-limited minimal disclosure, not as a privacy failure.

Rework "Each node pulls updates continuously from the centralized model."

Two different things are being conflated, on two very different clocks.

Instead: split the channels. Watchlist updates are pushed within seconds of a case being opened — that is where latency matters. Model updates follow scheduled federated rounds (nightly or weekly). Continuous federated training would be unstable and would learn nothing at your data rates.

Keep, scoped "Integrate SMPC and Differential Privacy."

Correct instinct, but full multi-party computation over a model is a research project on its own. Scope it: use pairwise-masked secure aggregation (Flower ships SecAgg+), which is a genuine SMPC construction and gives you exactly the property you want — the server sees only the sum, never any single node's update. Add DP-SGD via Opacus on the local head, and report a utility-versus-ε curve. Both are practical precisely because the payload is 2 MB, not 166 MB.

Keep — promote it "Maybe create a dashboard at nodes for manually verifying the match."

This is the best idea in your draft and you listed it as optional. It is not optional — it does three jobs at once: it is the false-positive control that makes the system operationally usable, it is the legal safeguard that keeps a human accountable for every disclosure, and it is the label source that makes federated learning possible at all. Make it a first-class component.

03

Revised problem statement and objectives

Submittable wording, with the scope tightened to what two semesters can deliver and defend.

Problem statement

Missing-person searches across distributed camera networks currently require either centralising privacy-sensitive video from every participating site, or abandoning cross-site collaboration entirely. Centralisation exposes the biometric data of the overwhelming majority of people captured — uninvolved bystanders who are not the subject of any search — and creates a single high-value target for inversion, membership-inference and gradient-leakage attacks. This project designs and evaluates a decentralised framework in which participating sites (hospitals, campuses, NGO shelters, transit hubs) collaboratively improve a shared surveillance-face-matching model without any raw frame, face crop, or biometric embedding ever leaving the site of capture.

Objectives

  1. Build an edge identification pipeline for degraded imagery. Detection, multi-object tracking, quality gating, and frozen-backbone face embedding, running end-to-end on a single edge device against surveillance-resolution video, with track-level rather than frame-level decisions — directly addressing the tracking gap left open by [1].
  2. Federate the components that genuinely benefit from it. A surveillance-domain adapter and a pairwise match verifier, trained across non-IID nodes using FedProx, with the recognition backbone frozen and shared. Demonstrate that the federated model beats every node's local-only model and approaches the centralised upper bound.
  3. Protect the model updates. Pairwise-masked secure aggregation so the server observes only the aggregate, plus DP-SGD with per-example clipping and Gaussian noise, reported as a measured privacy–utility curve rather than a single claimed ε.
  4. Protect the watchlist. Cancelable, per-node-keyed binary templates so that a compromised node's stored watchlist is revocable, unlinkable across sites, and resistant to reconstruction — with the accuracy cost of the transform measured, not assumed.
  5. Evaluate the way an operator would. Open-set identification metrics at fixed false-positive identification rates, plus alerts-per-camera-per-day at the chosen operating point — and an empirical attack evaluation (membership inference, embedding inversion, update poisoning).

Explicitly out of scope

Say this in the report; it protects you at the viva. Not in scope: training a face recognition backbone from scratch; gait, voice or multi-modal biometrics; cross-camera person re-identification beyond faces; blockchain-based aggregation; homomorphic encryption of full model updates; and any live deployment on non-consenting subjects.

05

System architecture

Two planes, on two clocks. The operational plane distributes watchlists and returns alerts in near-real time. The learning plane runs scheduled federated rounds. They share the node but nothing else.

Why the quality gate matters more than it looks

Stage A discards roughly 70% of detected faces before they ever reach the embedding model. This is not an optimisation detail — it is an accuracy mechanism. Face recognition on a 12-pixel, heavily blurred, 80°-yaw crop does not produce a weak match; it produces a confident wrong match. Rejecting those crops outright removes the largest single source of false alarms and cuts your edge compute budget by more than half at the same time. Report the gate's thresholds and its rejection rate as a result.

Training objectives at the node

Adapter. Cosine-embedding or triplet loss on pairs of (degraded surveillance crop, clean reference) for the same identity, with hard negatives mined from that node's own non-matching tracks. In the experimental setup each node holds both sides of the pair from its private partition; in the deployment story, the "clean" side is the watchlist probe itself.

Verifier. Binary cross-entropy on operator-confirmed labels, plus the mined hard negatives. Calibrate the output — an uncalibrated confidence score is useless to an operator deciding whether to act.

Aggregation

Use FedProx with a tuned proximal term rather than plain FedAvg. Paper [6] found precisely the failure mode you will hit — client drift and model divergence under non-IID partitions with DP noise — and found FedProx corrected it. Run FedAvg as your baseline so you can show the difference; that comparison is a result in itself.

The three-way comparison you must produce

Every FL claim needs all three numbers: local-only (each node trains alone — the lower bound), federated (your system), and centralised (all data pooled — the upper bound you are not allowed to reach). If federated does not beat local-only, the entire methodology is unjustified. Run this comparison in week 10, not week 24 — it is the single most important early result, and you need time to react if it comes out flat.

Three things in your literature that you should deliberately not build

Blockchain appears in [7], [12] and [13]. It solves aggregator trust — which secure aggregation already solves for your threat model — and it will consume an entire semester in tooling. Cite it as future work and move on. Homomorphic encryption of full updates ([5], [11]) is too slow; benchmark it once on your 2 MB head so you can justify the exclusion with a measured number rather than an opinion, which is a much stronger report section. Multi-modal biometrics (gait, voice, micro-expression as in [5], [8], [14]) doubles the project for no marginal contribution.

An optional SMPC demonstration worth the two weeks

If you want a second, sharper SMPC result beyond secure aggregation: implement two-party secure threshold comparison between a node and the server using additive secret sharing — the two parties jointly determine whether a match score exceeds the alert threshold without the node revealing the score and without the server revealing the threshold. It is a few hundred lines, it is a genuine MPC protocol, it benchmarks cleanly, and it demonstrates the technique on a small enough surface that it cannot sink the project. Paper [4]'s partial-SMPC approach — restricting secret sharing to a single layer rather than the whole model — is the right precedent to cite for this kind of deliberate scoping.

The degradation pipeline

This is what makes clean data useful, and it deserves to be a named contribution in your report. Take a clean CelebA or LFW face and push it through a physically-motivated chain: downsample to a target inter-ocular distance sampled from 16–48 px → directional motion blur → sensor noise → gamma shift for low light → JPEG at quality 30–70 → H.264 re-encode at a low bitrate. Sample the parameters per node so each node gets a distinct, consistent camera "personality" — that is what generates your non-IID partition.

Then validate the pipeline itself: a model trained only on synthetically degraded CelebA should transfer measurably to natively-degraded QMUL-SurvFace. If it does not, your synthetic chain is wrong and you need to know that in week 5, not week 22. Paper [16] is the right citation here, including its negative finding — excessive angular rotation degraded accuracy through facial distortion, so more augmentation is not automatically better.

Partitioning across nodes

Partition on two axes simultaneously, following [6]'s non-IID setup: disjoint identities (no person appears at two nodes, which is realistic and makes the FL problem honest) and distinct degradation profiles (each node's camera chain differs). Five to eight nodes. Also run one deliberately pathological partition — one node with 10× the data of the others — to show what happens to FedAvg and how FedProx handles it.

09

Evaluation — the part most capstones get wrong

Your literature reports numbers like 97.50% [1], 99.75% [2], 94.68% [4]. Those are balanced-set accuracies. They tell an operator nothing about whether the system is usable, and a sharp examiner will press on exactly this.

The base-rate problem, stated in your report's own numbers

One camera sees roughly 2,000 distinct people a day. Your watchlist holds 50 open cases. That is 100,000 comparisons per camera per day. At a false-positive identification rate of 10⁻³ per comparison — which would be an excellent number on any benchmark in your survey — you generate 100 false alarms per camera per day. The system is unusable. To get to roughly one false alarm per camera per day you need an alert-level FPIR near 10⁻⁵.

Two things close that gap: the quality gate, which removes the crops that generate confident-wrong matches, and track-level aggregation, which requires k of n frames in a track to agree. Note carefully that frames within a track are correlated, so the reduction is sub-geometric — you must measure it empirically, never assume independence. Getting this right, and saying it plainly, is the difference between a capstone that reports a benchmark number and one that reports an engineering result.

13

Ethics, law and governance

Include this as a full chapter, not an appendix. For a facial-recognition surveillance project it is a substantive part of the work, and examiners increasingly expect it.

Legal frame

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 governs digital personal data and applies to biometric processing; the Puttaswamy judgment established privacy as a fundamental right and requires that any intrusion be legal, necessary and proportionate. There is, as far as this report is aware, no dedicated Indian statute specifically governing facial recognition in surveillance — verify the current position when you write, since this area is moving. Your design should be able to point at concrete answers for consent, purpose limitation, data minimisation, retention and erasure, which the architecture in §05 already gives you.

Governance controls to specify explicitly

  • Purpose limitation by construction. A watchlist template is scoped to one case and carries a TTL. When the case closes, the template expires at every node automatically — nobody has to remember to delete it.
  • Human accountability for every disclosure. No image leaves a node without a named operator confirming it. The audit log records who, when and on what evidence.
  • Auditability in both directions. Node operators can inspect the log of what the server distributed to them and what they returned. Trust in these systems has to be mutual to be real.
  • False positives are the primary harm. An innocent person misidentified against a missing-person watchlist can be detained or approached by police. Design the operating point for this, and say in your report that you did.
  • Demographic performance. Report accuracy disaggregated by any demographic attribute your datasets label. Uneven error rates across groups are the best-documented failure mode in deployed face recognition, and a project that does not check is a project that missed something.

For the custom CCTV collection

Written informed consent from every participant, institutional approval before a single frame is recorded, no bystanders captured, encrypted and access-controlled storage, deletion at project end, and the dataset never published or committed to a repository. Include the consent form as an appendix — it is easy marks and it is the right thing to do.

Misuse considerations

Write a short honest section on how this architecture could be abused: the same system that finds a missing child finds a protester, and federation makes it easier to deploy at scale precisely because it lowers the legal and infrastructural barriers to participation. Name that tension. Explain which of your controls (case scoping, TTL, mutual audit, human gate) are aimed at it and which of them are policy rather than technical. Examiners respond very well to a team that can see the shape of its own work.

The empty cell—Open-set missing-person retrieval on surveillance-degraded video, using federated adaptation over a frozen backbone, learning from human-verified labels that cannot be centralised, secured with secure aggregation and DP, and evaluated at operationally honest false-positive rates rather than balanced-set accuracy.

Three parts of that sentence are individually defensible as contributions, which is what you want — the project does not stand or fall on any one of them. The frozen-backbone federated adaptation argument (federate the 0.5 M parameters that carry site-specific information; freeze the 43.6 M that do not). The human-verified label loop that turns operator review into the training signal, which is genuinely novel in this literature. And the operationally honest evaluation — reporting alerts-per-camera-per-day alongside TPIR@FPIR, when every paper in your survey reports balanced accuracy.
